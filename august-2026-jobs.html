August gave us the strongest jobs report we had seen in a while

Tobias Velez · August 2026 · 7 min read

August gave us the strongest jobs report we had seen in a while.

That mattered because the beginning of the summer made the labor market look pretty shaky. Instead, employers added 162,000 jobs in August. For comparison, job growth had averaged only 31,000 per month over the previous year. The unemployment rate stayed at 4.1 percent.

That is a pretty big bounce.

It also was not just one good-looking number. The separate household survey found that the number of employed Americans increased by 569,000 during August. The labor force grew by 683,000 people, and the labor force participation rate rose from 61.4 percent to 61.6 percent. Basically, more people came into the labor market looking for work, and a lot of them found jobs.

There was another encouraging detail that is easy to miss. The number of people working part time because they could not find full-time work or had their hours cut fell by 414,000 to 4.4 million.

Long-term unemployment was still a problem, though. About 1.9 million people had been unemployed for at least 27 weeks. They made up roughly 27 percent of all unemployed Americans. So even during a pretty good month, the job market was obviously not equally good for everyone.

Where the jobs actually showed up

Restaurants and bars added about 59,000 jobs, far above their average monthly gain of 12,000 over the previous year. Local government education added another 42,000. Manufacturing gained 16,000 jobs. Construction added about 22,000. Health care kept adding workers too, although its gain of about 13,000 was smaller than its average over the previous year.

Then there was information, which went in the opposite direction. The information industry lost 23,000 jobs in August. That included losses in computing infrastructure and data processing, publishing, and broadcasting. I thought that was interesting because these are industries sitting pretty close to the center of all the excitement around technology right now. A sector can attract massive investment and attention without automatically creating a huge number of jobs every month.

Wages increased too, but not incredibly fast. Average hourly earnings rose 10 cents to $37.75. That was a 0.3 percent increase during August and a 3.1 percent increase from a year earlier.

Businesses are busy, not eager to hire

So the basic jobs story was good. Then you look at businesses, and things get more complicated.

Manufacturing was still expanding in August. The ISM Manufacturing PMI was 54.6, down from 55.6 in July but still comfortably above 50. Production remained strong at 58.3, while new orders came in at 53.7. Manufacturing employment was still just above the expansion line at 51.2.

Services looked even stronger. The Services PMI rose from 54.1 to 55.4. Business activity jumped to 61.7, its highest level since November 2022. New orders reached 60.9, the highest reading since February 2023. Basically, customers were placing orders and businesses were busy.

But those businesses were still not hiring very aggressively. The services employment index was only 47.8, its second straight month below 50. About 17.1 percent of the businesses responding to the survey reported lower employment, although that was slightly better than the 19 percent that reported cuts in July.

There were also two comments buried in the report that I thought captured the situation really well. One company said it had a bottleneck in IT projects because it did not have enough software development workers. A different company said it needed more employees but was under a hiring freeze. Those are two separate businesses, but together they describe something weird that can happen in the economy: a company can have plenty of work to do and still decide that it does not want to hire.

The part of the story we’re still waiting on

This is the part of August I would probably worry about most. The manufacturing prices index was 71.1, unchanged from July. The services prices index climbed from 70.3 to 72.6. Fifteen service industries reported higher prices paid, while not a single industry reported an overall decrease. Again, 72.6 does not mean prices literally increased 72.6 percent; it means price increases were extremely widespread among the companies in the survey. Manufacturers were reporting higher costs for things like aluminum, copper, diesel fuel, freight, semiconductors and several different kinds of steel.

We still do not know exactly how much of those higher business costs reached consumers. As of September 5, the official August CPI report has not been released. It comes out September 11. August retail sales come later in the month, and the August PCE inflation report will not arrive until the end of September. That means any August summary written right now has to admit that part of the story is still missing.

What people are actually worried about

We do know consumers were worried. The University of Michigan Consumer Sentiment Index dropped from 55.2 in July to 51.7 in August. Current economic conditions fell to 51.9 and expectations fell to 51.5. Sentiment was around 11 percent lower than a year earlier. The decline was especially large among lower- and middle-income households, older consumers and people without college degrees.

One result from the survey stood out to me more than anything else. When people were asked whether inflation or unemployment was causing the more serious hardship, 36 percent chose inflation. Only 6 percent chose unemployment. At the beginning of the year, those numbers were 23 percent and 14 percent. That gap says a lot about August.

On paper, the labor market looked pretty good. Unemployment was low. Hiring bounced back. Factories were expanding. Service businesses were getting more orders. But people do not experience “the economy” as a jobs report. They experience it when they buy groceries, fill up a car, pay rent, or look at how much money is left in their account at the end of the month.

That is what makes the next inflation report so important. If inflation cooled while the labor market was adding 162,000 jobs, August will look like a genuinely strong month. If prices stayed hot, then the Fed has a much harder problem.

For now, August gave us one pretty clear piece of good news. People were getting hired again. Whether the rest of the economy cooperated is something we still do not fully know.

Data: Bureau of Labor Statistics (jobs), Institute for Supply Management, University of Michigan Surveys of Consumers, Federal Reserve.
Note on dates: written September 5, 2026. The official August CPI report had not yet been released at the time of writing; it was scheduled for September 11.
