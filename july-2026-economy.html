July 2026: the economy looked different depending on which number you opened first

Tobias Velez · July 2026 · 7 min read

July was one of those months where the economy looked completely different depending on which number you opened first.

Jobs looked weak. Factories looked surprisingly strong. People were making more money, but they were barely spending more after inflation. Homebuilding dropped pretty sharply. The trade deficit jumped. Inflation itself was calmer during the month, although energy prices were still causing problems.

There was no one number that really summed July up.

The jobs number that changed twice

The jobs report is probably the best place to start because the number changed quite a bit after it first came out. The original estimate said the U.S. lost 23,000 jobs in July. A month later, after the Bureau of Labor Statistics received more responses from businesses, July was revised up by 44,000 jobs. The current estimate is that the economy actually added 21,000 jobs.

That is obviously better than losing jobs, but 21,000 is still tiny for the entire U.S. economy. Unemployment was also still pretty low at 4.1 percent. So July was not really a month where huge numbers of people suddenly lost their jobs. It looked more like companies were becoming much more careful about hiring.

The JOLTS report makes that clearer. There were about 7.3 million open jobs at the end of July, but businesses made only about 5.1 million hires during the month. Around 3.1 million workers quit their jobs, and the quits rate was 1.9 percent. Professional and business services alone made 188,000 fewer hires than the month before.

I think the quits number is especially useful. People are usually more comfortable quitting when they think they can find another job pretty easily. When that slows down, it can be a sign that workers are less confident about what is waiting for them on the other side.

Inflation eased, gas didn’t

Inflation was a little more encouraging. The Consumer Price Index rose just 0.1 percent in July. Compared with a year earlier, prices were up 3.4 percent. Core inflation, which takes out food and energy because their prices move around a lot, rose 0.2 percent during the month and 2.5 percent over the year.

Housing costs were still important. Shelter prices rose 0.1 percent in July and accounted for around two thirds of the overall monthly increase.

Energy was the much uglier part. Gasoline prices were 24.6 percent higher than a year earlier, helping push the overall energy index up 14.7 percent. So someone hearing that inflation had cooled could still go fill up their car and reasonably wonder what everyone was talking about.

The Fed’s preferred inflation measure, PCE, told a similar story. Prices rose 0.2 percent in July and were 3.7 percent higher than a year earlier. Core PCE was also up 0.2 percent for the month and 3.3 percent over the year. That is still well above the Federal Reserve’s 2 percent target.

Income rose, real spending didn’t

What I found more interesting in the PCE report was what people actually did with their money. Personal income rose 0.4 percent in July and disposable income rose 0.5 percent. Consumer spending rose 0.2 percent before accounting for inflation. After inflation, though, real consumer spending basically did not move. People spent more on services, but less on goods, and the personal saving rate was only 3.0 percent.

Retail sales showed something similar. Americans spent about $763.6 billion at retailers and restaurants in July, which was down 0.6 percent from June. Sales were still 5.0 percent higher than a year earlier, so consumers had not suddenly stopped buying things. July was just noticeably weaker than the month before.

Housing looked rougher

Housing starts fell 12.4 percent in July to an annual rate of about 1.24 million homes. Single-family starts fell 9.9 percent. At the same time, building permits actually rose 5.0 percent, which could mean builders were still planning more construction later even though fewer homes actually got started during July.

New-home sales fell 10.5 percent to an annual rate of 607,000. Builders ended July with around 488,000 new homes for sale, enough to last 9.6 months at the current sales pace. The median new home sold for $393,800.

Factories told a different story

Then you get to manufacturing, and the story changes again. The ISM Manufacturing PMI reached 55.6 in July. A reading above 50 generally means manufacturing is expanding. More importantly, 55.6 was the strongest reading since May 2022. Production rose to 58.5, new orders reached 56.7, and manufacturing employment moved above 50 for the first time in 33 months. Four of the six largest manufacturing industries expanded.

Services were growing too. The Services PMI was 54.1. Business activity came in at 59.1 and new orders at 57.2.

But there was a strange detail underneath those numbers. The services employment index fell to 47.4, which means service businesses were reporting less employment even while business activity and new orders were growing. That fits pretty well with what the regular jobs data were showing. Companies could still have plenty of work without feeling much urgency to hire.

Costs were another warning sign. The services prices index reached 70.3, while the manufacturing prices index was 71.1. Those numbers do not mean prices rose 70 percent; they measure how widespread price increases are among the businesses answering the survey. Still, both readings were very high.

A bigger trade gap, and why

Then there was trade. The U.S. trade deficit jumped from $71.2 billion in June to $88.6 billion in July. Imports rose to $399.3 billion while exports fell to $310.7 billion. Imports of capital goods reached a record $140.3 billion, and imports from Mexico and Taiwan also hit records.

That jump is interesting because capital goods include things businesses use to produce other things, like computers and equipment. Imports of computers rose by $6.9 billion in July and computer accessories rose by another $6.6 billion. So part of the bigger trade deficit came from American businesses buying a lot more equipment from abroad, not just consumers buying more imported stuff.

By the end of the month, the Federal Reserve was basically stuck with the same confusing picture everyone else was. It kept its main interest rate between 3.5 and 3.75 percent. Inflation was still too high to ignore, but weaker hiring gave the Fed another reason to be careful about keeping rates high for too long.

That is probably the best way to describe July. People were not suddenly getting laid off everywhere, but hiring was weak. Consumers had more income, but real spending barely moved. Factories were busy, but housing was struggling. Inflation looked calmer for one month, while businesses were still reporting higher costs.

July was not a recession month. It also was not a particularly comfortable one. Mostly, it showed why saying the economy is simply “good” or “bad” usually misses half the story.

Data: Bureau of Labor Statistics (jobs, JOLTS, CPI), Bureau of Economic Analysis (PCE, trade), U.S. Census Bureau (retail sales, housing), Institute for Supply Management, Federal Reserve.
