Standards

How I source and check this

I’m a high school student, not a newsroom. That means the sourcing rules have to be stricter, not looser, because nobody is checking my work before it goes up. This page says what those rules are and lists what I have gotten wrong.

Where figures come from

Every number comes from a primary source where one exists. In practice that means the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the Federal Reserve, the Energy Information Administration, the Census Bureau, Montgomery Planning, and MCPS publications including the capital improvements program. Each article ends with a data line naming its sources, and where a figure is contested between sources I name the specific series I used, because two credible sources can report different numbers for the same month and quietly picking one is how errors survive.

When the calculation is mine

Some findings here are not published anywhere. The school-by-school MCPS capacity breakdown is arithmetic I did myself from Appendix E of the capital improvements program: enrollment minus listed capacity, per school, summed separately for schools over and under. Where that happens, the article says so explicitly and describes the method, and the underlying data is published as an open dataset so anyone can reproduce or contradict it. An original calculation that nobody can check is not evidence.

What I do when I cannot verify something

I remove it, and I say that I removed it. An unverifiable claim that supports my argument is more dangerous than one that undercuts it, because I am less likely to look hard at it, and leaving a shaky number in because it makes the piece stronger is the failure mode I am most trying to avoid.

Corrections

If something here is wrong, email me. I will fix the article and add the change to the log below rather than editing quietly. I would rather be corrected than be wrong, and a visible correction log is the only thing that makes that claim mean anything.

A draft stated the county’s Gini coefficient rose from 0.42 to 0.46. I could not confirm a consistent primary source and time period, so the claim was cut, with a note in the article explaining the removal.

MarchCorrected

An earlier version used Brent crude at roughly $94 on March 9, which understated conditions at the March 17–18 FOMC meeting. Corrected to $108 on March 17 and $118 on March 18, from the EIA daily Europe Brent Spot Price FOB series.

AprilClarified

Two EIA shut-in figures for April appeared without explanation. They are not contradictory: 9.1 million barrels per day was the forecast in the April outlook, and 10.5 million was the later estimate once April had ended. The article now distinguishes the two.

JuneCorrected

An earlier version gave the average credit-card rate as “around 21%,” which covers all accounts. Because the passage discusses someone carrying a balance, it now uses 22.15%, the rate on accounts actually assessed interest, from the Federal Reserve’s July 8 release.

“Restaurant meals” replaced with “eating out” to match the BLS category actually cited, food away from home.
