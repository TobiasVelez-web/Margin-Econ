Southwest gave up its most famous feature, and the reason is basic economics

Tobias Velez · August 2026 · 8 min read

For more than 50 years, Southwest had one feature that almost everybody knew about even if they barely flew.

There were no assigned seats. You checked in, got a boarding position, stood by those weird numbered poles at the gate, and hoped nobody had already taken the seat you wanted. That system became part of Southwest’s identity.

Then Southwest killed it.

Since January 27, 2026, the airline has used assigned seating. There are now Standard seats, Preferred seats near the front, and Extra Legroom seats. People buying Southwest’s cheapest Basic tickets do not even choose their regular seat when they book. It gets assigned at check-in unless they pay for something better.

This is interesting to me because Southwest did not just change how people get onto an airplane. It changed how it makes money.

A simple product gets more pieces

For years, Southwest had an unusually simple product. You bought a flight. Everybody sat in basically the same cabin. Two checked bags were free. There were different fares and boarding benefits, but compared with most airlines, Southwest did not divide the trip into nearly as many little things you could pay extra for.

Now it does. A customer can pay for a better seat, a better location, more legroom, earlier boarding, a more expensive fare, or checked luggage, or none of those things. The flight still gets everybody from the same airport to the same destination. Southwest has just found more pieces of that trip that different customers value differently.

Before making the change, the company did a huge amount of research. Southwest said 80 percent of its own customers preferred assigned seating. Among people who usually flew other airlines, the number was 86 percent. Even more important, Southwest said its seating and boarding system was the number one reason former customers gave for not coming back. That creates a pretty obvious problem for a company: something can make your brand different without actually making customers like it.

Redesigning the plane without losing speed

Southwest still had another problem to solve. Open seating was fast, and a big part of Southwest’s old business model was getting planes turned around quickly. If assigned seats made boarding dramatically slower, the airline could gain money from better seats but lose some of it through delays and less efficient operations.

So Southwest tested the idea before changing the whole airline, using live boarding trials and more than 8 million computer simulations to study what would happen. Then it redesigned the cabin. Roughly one third of the seats were planned as extra-legroom seats, with as much as five additional inches of space depending on the aircraft. On some planes, Southwest could make that work without removing any seats. On its smaller Boeing 737-700s, it could not. Those planes lost an entire row, or six seats.

That sounds like a terrible way for an airline to make more money, since fewer seats means fewer tickets to sell. But this is where the economics gets interesting.

The math behind removing seats

Imagine Southwest can fit six extra passengers on a plane and make $100 from each of them. That is $600. Now imagine it removes those six seats but gets 30 other passengers to pay an extra $30 because they really want more legroom or a better location. That is $900.

Those are just made-up numbers to show the idea, not Southwest’s actual pricing. But the logic is real: a company does not necessarily want to sell the largest possible number of products. It wants to make the most money from the products it sells.

Economists call part of this price discrimination. The phrase sounds worse than it is. It basically means finding ways to charge different customers different amounts based on what they are willing to pay.

An aisle seat in row five and a middle seat in row twenty eight both arrive in Orlando at the exact same second. They are still not equally valuable to everyone. Someone who is six foot four might happily pay extra for legroom. Someone with a short connection might care a lot about sitting near the front. A family might pay more to know that everybody can sit together. A student trying to get home as cheaply as possible might take literally any seat on the plane. Those people are buying the same basic flight, but they do not value every part of it the same way, and Southwest can now make money from those differences.

Turning one product into several

Its new fare system does this directly. Basic customers get a Standard seat assigned at check-in. Choice customers can select a Standard seat when they book. Choice Preferred includes Preferred seating. Choice Extra includes Extra Legroom seating. Technically, Southwest still has one economy cabin. Economically, it has created several different products inside that cabin.

Boarding has become another product too. Southwest now uses Groups 1 through 8. People with Extra Legroom seats and more expensive fares generally get better boarding positions, and customers can also pay separately for Priority Boarding on eligible flights.

Then there are bags, which might be the strangest part of Southwest’s transformation. In 2024, the company told investors that its own research showed free checked bags were the most important thing separating Southwest from competitors. It even argued that charging for bags could hurt demand enough to cancel out the money earned from bag fees. Then it changed course. Today, most Basic, Choice and Choice Preferred passengers on mainland U.S. flights pay for checked bags. Choice Extra passengers still get two free bags, and some loyalty members and Southwest credit card holders can also qualify for free bags.

A bag fee is not just a bag fee. It can make a more expensive ticket look better, make airline status more useful, or give someone another reason to sign up for a Southwest credit card. Something that used to come automatically with every ticket can now help Southwest sell other products.

The strategy is already showing up in the numbers

There is already evidence that the broader strategy is making a real financial difference. In the second quarter of 2026, Southwest reported a company record $8.4 billion in operating revenue, up 16.4 percent from the same quarter a year earlier, and passenger revenue increased 16.9 percent.

The really interesting number, though, is capacity. Southwest increased flight capacity by only 0.2 percent. So revenue did not jump 16 percent because Southwest suddenly put 16 percent more seats into the sky. Revenue per available seat mile increased 16.2 percent and passenger yield rose 15.4 percent. In simple terms, Southwest was making much more revenue from roughly the same amount of flying.

Southwest said that increase came mainly from more customers buying higher fare categories under its new fare system, along with revenue from newer extras like bag fees, assigned seats and extra-legroom seats. There were other reasons too, including strong demand, so it would be wrong to give every dollar of revenue growth to the new strategy. But the company itself says these changes are a major part of what is happening.

The strategy is reaching beyond normal leisure travelers too. Southwest said managed business revenue rose 30 percent from a year earlier during the second quarter. Rapid Rewards was approaching 100 million members. New loyalty enrollments were up 35 percent, and new credit card acquisitions were up 28 percent.

Southwest is no longer just trying to sell you a seat from Baltimore to Orlando. It wants you choosing a higher fare, paying for the right seat, using its credit card, joining its loyalty program, checking a bag, and boarding earlier. Each of those gives Southwest another chance to make money from the same customer.

None of this happened by accident. When Southwest first laid out the seating overhaul, it estimated that assigned and premium seating could eventually create around $1.5 billion in additional annual operating profit by 2027. That is a company forecast, not a guarantee, but it shows how much money management thought was hiding inside something as simple as deciding who sits where.

What’s actually changed

People usually talk about airline fees like they are just companies finding annoying new ways to take another twenty or fifty dollars from you. Sometimes that is part of it. But underneath all of those fees is a much bigger economic strategy.

For years, Southwest sold an unusually simple product to almost everybody. That simplicity made the airline recognizable, but it also meant leaving money on the table whenever one customer cared much more about something than another customer did. Now Southwest is trying to figure out exactly what each passenger values and charge accordingly.

The funny part is that the actual product barely changed. It is still a Southwest 737 going to the same city. What changed is how many different things Southwest figured out it could sell you before you get there.
